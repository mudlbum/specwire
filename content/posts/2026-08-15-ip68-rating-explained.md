Apple rates iPhone 12 through iPhone 17 at **IP68 under IEC standard 60529, to a maximum depth of 6 metres for up to 30 minutes**. Samsung rates its IP68 Galaxy devices at **1.5 metres for up to 30 minutes**.

Both labels are accurate. Both say IP68. One describes protection at four times the depth of the other.

That is the single most useful thing to know about IP ratings: the badge is a family, not a figure. And even within one manufacturer it splits — Apple certifies three different depths across devices all wearing the same IP68 mark. If you are comparing two phones on water resistance, the badge tells you almost nothing. The depth in the manufacturer's own documentation tells you everything.

## What do the two digits actually mean?

The IP code comes from IEC 60529, the international standard for how well an enclosure keeps things out. Two digits, two separate jobs.

**The first digit is solids**, on a scale to 6. A 6 means dust-tight — no ingress at all. This part is unambiguous, and it is why almost every flagship phone shows a 6 in the first position.

**The second digit is water**, and this is where it gets slippery. Ratings up to 7 have fixed conditions: IPX7 means immersion to 1 metre for 30 minutes. But 8 means only *continuous immersion beyond* what 7 requires, with the actual conditions declared by the manufacturer and stated in the product documentation.

So an 8 is not a fixed depth. It is a promise that the manufacturer tested deeper than 1 metre and wrote down how deep.

> [!KEY] The **6** in IP68 is a fixed, defined dust rating. The **8** is a pointer to a number the manufacturer publishes separately. Read that number.

## How far apart do the real figures get?

Far enough to matter. Here is what the two largest phone makers publish, from their own support pages:

| Device | Rating | Certified conditions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| iPhone 12 – iPhone 17, iPhone Air | IP68 | 6 metres, up to 30 minutes |
| iPhone 11 Pro / Pro Max | IP68 | 4 metres, up to 30 minutes |
| iPhone 11, XS, XS Max | IP68 | 2 metres, up to 30 minutes |
| Samsung Galaxy (IP68 models) | IP68 | 1.5 metres fresh water, up to 30 minutes |
| iPhone SE (2nd/3rd gen), XR, X, 8, 7 | IP67 | 1 metre, up to 30 minutes |

Three of those rows are the same company, the same badge, and depths of 6, 4 and 2 metres. A buyer comparing an iPhone 11 against an iPhone 15 on the spec sheet sees "IP68" twice and no difference at all.

Note also how close the bottom rows sit. A Galaxy at IP68 is rated to 1.5 metres; an iPhone SE at IP67 is rated to 1 metre. The badges look a tier apart. The tested depths are 50 centimetres apart.

## What do these tests deliberately not cover?

This is the part that ends badly for people, and both manufacturers are unusually direct about it.

**Still water only.** Samsung's guidance says not to expose the device to water moving with force. Apple's list of things to avoid includes showering, water skiing, wakeboarding, surfing and jet skiing, and explicitly names pressurised or high-velocity water. An immersion test measures a device sitting in a tank. A shower head is a different physical event.

**Fresh water only.** Samsung says that if the device meets any liquid other than fresh water, you should rinse it gently with fresh water immediately and dry it, and warns that not doing so may cause operability or cosmetic problems. Apple separately asks you to minimise contact with soap, detergent, acids, perfume, insect repellent, lotions, sunscreen, oil and hair dye. Chlorine and salt are not in the test and not in the rating.

**A new device only.** Apple states that splash, water and dust resistance are not permanent conditions and that resistance might decrease as a result of normal wear. Samsung warns that if the device is dropped or receives an impact, the water and dust-resistant features may be damaged. Nobody re-tests your phone after a year in a pocket.

> [!WARNING] Apple states directly that **liquid damage is not covered under warranty**, while noting you may have separate rights under consumer law. An IP68 rating and warranty coverage for water damage are unrelated things.

That combination — a rating earned on a factory-fresh unit in still, clean water, with no warranty behind it — is the gap between what the badge suggests and what you actually own.

## So what is the rating good for?

Quite a lot, as long as you read it as insurance against accidents rather than a licence for activities.

Rain, a spilled drink, a dropped phone in a sink, a wet countertop, a walk home in a downpour: this is what the rating covers well, and it covers it far better than phones did a decade ago. Apple even lists a set of models as resistant to accidental spills of soda, beer, coffee, tea and juice, which is a specific and useful claim.

What it is not is a swimming rating. Or a shower rating. Or a promise that holds in year three.

> [!ACTION] Practical rules that follow from the documentation
> 1. **Look up your exact model**, not the badge. Two phones with identical IP68 labels can differ by metres.
> 2. **Rinse with fresh water** after any contact with salt water, chlorine, or anything that is not water. Then dry it properly.
> 3. **Wait before charging.** Apple advises allowing at least 5 hours before connecting a cable or accessory after liquid exposure, and not charging while wet.
> 4. **Treat a drop as a reset.** Impacts can compromise the seals even when the screen survives, and nothing will warn you.
> 5. **Ask about sealing after any repair.** Reopening a phone disturbs the adhesive that creates the rating.
> 6. **Never dry it with heat or cotton buds.** Apple specifically warns against external heat sources and inserting anything into the port.

## The habit worth building

IP68 is one of the better-behaved specifications in consumer tech. It comes from a real international standard, the dust digit is unambiguous, and both major manufacturers publish their exact test conditions in public where anyone can read them.

The failure is not in the standard. It is that the badge gets quoted and the conditions do not — so two products with genuinely different protection end up looking identical in a comparison table.

Which is the same habit that makes every other spec sheet readable: find the condition attached to the number. Depth and duration for an IP rating. State of charge and adapter for a charging claim. Test patch and duration for a brightness claim — which is exactly why [a DisplayHDR 1000 badge does not mean 1,000 nits on screen](/displays/displayhdr-tiers-explained/), and why [an HDMI 2.1 port can carry a third of the bandwidth you assumed](/explainers/4k-144hz-hdmi-cable/).

Sustained power for a core count, too, which is how [a 16-core laptop chip ends up slower than an 8-core desktop](/computers/laptop-cores-vs-desktop-cores/). Link strength for a Bluetooth bitrate, which is how [LDAC's 990 kbps headline turns into 330 kbps on most phones](/audio/does-ldac-actually-sound-better-than-aac/).

The number alone is marketing. The number with its condition is a fact.
