The real difference isn't performance. It's disclosure.

Qualcomm publishes a three-page product brief for the Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5: two prime cores at up to 4.74 GHz, six performance cores at up to 3.62 GHz, a 3 nm process, LPDDR5X up to 5,300 MHz, UFS 4.1 storage, part numbers, and a modem rated to 12.5 Gbps down. Apple describes the A19 Pro on its own iPhone 17 Pro tech specs page in five lines, none of which contains a clock speed, a process node or a memory figure. So when you ask which chip is faster, you are asking a question that neither spec sheet can answer — and that independent measurement answers with "they're within about ten percent, and your phone's cooling matters more."

## What does each company actually put in writing?

Both documents were read on 3 September 2026. Here is the entire published comparison, side by side.

| What you'd want to know | Qualcomm, Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5 | Apple, A19 Pro |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CPU core layout | 2 prime + 6 performance, 64-bit | 6-core: 2 performance + 4 efficiency |
| Peak clock | 4.74 GHz (also sold at 4.6 GHz) | not published |
| Process node | "3nm Process Technology" | not published |
| GPU | Adreno, Vulkan 1.3 / OpenCL 3.0 FP | 6-core with Neural Accelerators |
| NPU | Hexagon, 12 scalar + 8 vector + 1 accelerator; INT2–INT16, FP8, FP16 | 16-core Neural Engine |
| NPU throughput (TOPS) | not published | not published |
| Memory | LPDDR5X up to 5,300 MHz, up to 24 GB | not published |
| Storage interface | UFS 4.1 | not published |
| Modem peak | 12.5 Gbps down, 3.7 Gbps up | not published |
| Wi-Fi peak | 5.8 Gbps (FastConnect 7900) | Wi-Fi 7, no rate given |
| Part number | SM8850-AC, SM8850-1-AD | not published |

Apple's launch release goes slightly further than the spec page and still avoids numbers: the A19 Pro has "a larger cache, and more memory than A18 Pro". Larger and more than what, exactly, is left to the reader.

The modem row is worth a second look, because 12.5 Gbps is a laboratory ceiling no carrier hands a phone. Where Apple *does* publish radio detail is the band list, and that list changes by region — which is the whole reason [mmWave 5G may be irrelevant where you live regardless of coverage](/phones/does-5g-mmwave-matter-where-you-live/).

> [!KEY]
> Qualcomm's page carries two footnotes that matter more than most of its headline claims. The platform is "also available in 4.6 GHz CPU version", and it is "also available in 7-core CPU version". Qualcomm's own advice: consult OEM specifications for device CPU speed.

## Are the two chips actually close?

Close enough that the argument is mostly tribal. Notebookcheck runs Geekbench 6.7.1 on every phone it reviews and publishes the range as well as the average, which is the useful part.

| Notebookcheck's own Geekbench 6.7.1 results | Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5 (23 devices) | Apple A19 Pro (4 devices) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Single-core, average | 3,631 | 3,840 |
| Single-core, range | 3,462 – 3,831 | 3,785 – 3,888 |
| Multi-core, average | 10,940 | 9,932 |
| Multi-core, range | 9,631 – 12,383 | 9,509 – 10,105 |

Apple takes single-core by roughly 6%. Qualcomm takes multi-core by roughly 10%, which is a modest return on 33% more cores — the extra ones are the slower kind, and sustained multi-core work hits the phone's thermal ceiling long before it runs out of cores to use. Notebookcheck's iPhone 17 Pro Max review unit posted 3,883 and 10,017 points, near the top of Apple's range in one test and the middle of it in the other.

None of those gaps is visible while you're using a phone. A 6% single-core difference is a few milliseconds on an app launch.

## Why does the Snapdragon number move so much more?

Because "Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5" describes a chip Qualcomm sells to two dozen companies, and "A19 Pro" describes a chip Apple sells to itself.

Across those 23 Snapdragon devices, Notebookcheck's multi-core results run from 9,631 to 12,383 — a 29% spread on one chip name. Apple's four devices span 9,509 to 10,105, about 6%. Part of that is simple arithmetic: four samples cannot spread as far as 23. But part of it is real, and it's the same effect we picked apart in [why two phones with the same Snapdragon score 40% apart](/phones/same-chip-different-benchmark-scores/) — different clock bins, different core counts, and wildly different cooling.

> [!WARNING]
> A review's benchmark number belongs to the review unit, not to the chip. Notebookcheck's slowest Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5 phone and its fastest are separated by more than the entire gap between Qualcomm and Apple. If you're choosing between two Android flagships, the chip name has told you almost nothing.

Apple's narrower range is a consequence of vertical integration rather than superior engineering. One company picks the silicon bin, designs the chassis, writes the thermal governor and sets the power limit. Qualcomm sells a platform and hands all four of those decisions to somebody else, which is why the same part turns up in a 250 g gaming phone with a vapour chamber and in a folding phone with nowhere to put the heat.

## What is Apple's "40 percent better sustained performance" measuring?

Nothing you can reproduce. Apple's September 2025 release states that A19 Pro, "when paired with the Apple-designed vapor chamber", enables up to 40% better sustained performance than the previous generation. The footnote hanging off that paragraph turns out to be about a game's release date. No workload is named, no run length, no starting temperature, no ambient.

Qualcomm's equivalents are no better as measurements, but they're at least labelled as marketing: 20% CPU performance, 35% CPU power efficiency, 23% GPU performance, all against the previous Snapdragon 8 Elite, all under a blanket "results will vary, depending on OEM implementation and other factors".

> [!NOTE]
> Both figures are legitimate engineering claims and neither is a specification. The difference between a claim and a spec is a stated test condition. "Up to 40% better sustained performance" has none; "2 prime cores, up to 4.74 GHz" has one implied and a footnote telling you when it doesn't apply.

This is the pattern across the whole category. Apple ships a genuinely excellent thermal design and describes it with an adjective. Qualcomm ships a documented part and buries the caveats in footnote 2. You end up needing third-party measurement either way — the same reason a display's brightness claim needs [nits, cd/m² and lumens pulled apart](/explainers/nits-cd-m2-lumens-explained/) before it means anything. Apple lists 3,000 nits peak outdoor brightness for the iPhone 17 Pro; Notebookcheck measured a maximum of 2,344 cd/m² on its test unit. Both can be true, because neither party states the same test patch.

## Which numbers survive contact with a shop?

Three, and none of them is the chip name.

The clock speed **your** phone maker publishes, not the one Qualcomm's platform page leads with. If the manufacturer won't state it, assume the 4.6 GHz seven-core part until told otherwise. For iPhones there is nothing to check, which is at least consistent.

The stress-test stability figure from a lab that ran one. A 3DMark or Wild Life run repeated for twenty minutes tells you what the phone does after the benchmark screenshot, and it is the single most predictive number for gaming or long camera sessions — the same relationship between rated power and delivered power that decides [why a 16-core laptop CPU loses to an 8-core desktop](/computers/laptop-cores-vs-desktop-cores/).

Memory and storage, which the chip only sets a ceiling for. Qualcomm's brief supports up to 24 GB of LPDDR5X and UFS 4.1; Notebookcheck's Snapdragon devices ship with 12 GB or 16 GB, and Apple publishes no RAM figure at all.

> [!ACTION]
> Before you compare two flagships: find the OEM's stated CPU clock and core count, not the platform's; find one lab's sustained-load result for each phone; check the RAM and storage tier you're actually buying; and treat every "up to X% faster" as a direction rather than a measurement.

The honest summary is that the Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5 and the A19 Pro are peers, that Qualcomm will tell you far more about its part than Apple will about theirs, and that neither disclosure predicts which of two phones will still be fast in twenty minutes. That last question has never been answered on a spec sheet, by anybody.
