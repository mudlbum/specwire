Your monitor is rated for 144Hz. Your graphics card drives 144Hz. You plugged in an HDMI cable, opened display settings, and the refresh dropdown stops dead at 60.

Here is the short answer. 4K at 144Hz needs roughly 28.7 Gbps of active video data in 8-bit colour, and a lot more once transmission overhead is added. Three things sit between your GPU and your panel — the source port, the cable, and the display's input — and **the link runs at the speed of the weakest one**. The cable is the part everyone blames and usually the part that is fine. The port is usually the problem, because a port badged "HDMI 2.1" is allowed to carry 18 Gbps and say nothing further about it.

So before you buy anything, work out which of the three is actually short.

## How much data does 4K 144Hz really need?

Start with the arithmetic, because it is arithmetic you can check.

A 3840 x 2160 frame holds 8,294,400 pixels. At 144 frames per second that is 1,194,393,600 pixels every second. In 8-bit RGB each pixel is 24 bits, so:

**1,194,393,600 x 24 = about 28.7 Gbps**, just for the visible picture.

Push to 10-bit colour for HDR and the same maths gives about 35.8 Gbps. Neither figure includes blanking intervals or the encoding overhead the link itself adds, both of which push the real requirement higher still. If you are chasing HDR as well as refresh rate, it is worth knowing [what each DisplayHDR tier actually requires in cd/m²](/displays/displayhdr-tiers-explained/) before you spend anything — the badge and the bandwidth are separate problems.

Now put that against what the cable tiers are certified to carry. These come straight from HDMI Licensing Administrator, the body that runs the certification programme:

| Cable name | Certified for | What HDMI LA lists it as handling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standard HDMI Cable | 1080i / 720p | Older consumer gear |
| High Speed HDMI Cable | 10.2 Gbps | 1080p, 4K@30Hz, 3D, Deep Color |
| Premium High Speed HDMI Cable | 18 Gbps | 4K60, HDR, BT.2020 colour |
| Ultra High Speed HDMI Cable | 48 Gbps | 8K@60 and 4K@120 |
| Ultra96 HDMI Cable | 96 Gbps | Uncompressed 8K@60/4:4:4, 4K@240/4:4:4, 10-bit and 12-bit |

Read the fourth row again. The Ultra High Speed cable — the 48 Gbps one, the best you could buy for years — is described by HDMI LA as handling **4K at 120Hz**. Not 144.

> [!KEY] 4K 144Hz sits above the format list HDMI publishes for its own 48 Gbps cable. When it works, something in the chain is compressing the signal.

That something is almost always DSC.

## What is DSC, and why does 4K 144Hz depend on it?

Display Stream Compression is a VESA standard that squeezes the video stream so it fits down a link that could not otherwise carry it. VESA's own figures for DSC 1.2a are a **3:1** compression ratio at 24-bit colour and **3.75:1** at 30-bit colour.

Run 28.7 Gbps through 3:1 and you are asking the link for under 10 Gbps. That fits almost anywhere. This is the mechanism that makes 4K 144Hz, 4K 240Hz and 8K 60Hz possible on hardware whose raw bandwidth says otherwise.

VESA describes DSC as visually lossless, and is specific about what that means: the claim is validated by subjective testing with human viewers rather than by mathematical identity. The compressed image is not bit-for-bit the original. It is engineered so you cannot tell, and VESA runs image-quality testing with panels of test subjects to check that holds.

DSC 1.2a was adopted into HDMI 2.1 and DisplayPort 2.0, so both interfaces can use it. The practical catch is that DSC has to be supported at **both** ends. A monitor that supports DSC and a graphics card that supports DSC will negotiate it silently. Miss it at either end and the link falls back to whatever raw bandwidth allows — which is where your 60Hz ceiling comes from.

> [!NOTE] DSC engaging is not a fault. On most 4K high-refresh monitors it is the intended operating mode, and you will never see a notification telling you it happened.

## Why is a port labelled HDMI 2.1 sometimes only 18 Gbps?

Because the version number was never a bandwidth guarantee, and HDMI Forum designed it that way.

When HDMI Forum released version 2.1 in November 2017, it raised the ceiling to 48 Gbps and introduced the Ultra High Speed cable to carry it. But the higher bandwidth, along with VRR, ALLM, eARC and the rest, was made **optional**. Manufacturers are required to state which features a given device supports; they are not required to implement any particular one to use the version number.

The result is a spec sheet that can say "HDMI 2.1" with complete accuracy on a port carrying 18 Gbps — the same bandwidth an HDMI 2.0 port carried. Nothing is being faked. The label simply does not mean what shoppers reasonably assume it means.

HDMI 2.2 repeats the pattern with a new twist. HDMI LA defines **"Ultra96" as a feature name for products supporting a maximum of 64, 80 or 96 Gbps**. A product can carry the Ultra96 name at two thirds of the number in the name.

> [!WARNING] Treat "HDMI 2.1", "HDMI 2.2" and "Ultra96" as families, not figures. The only claim worth reading is a stated Gbps number, or a stated maximum resolution and refresh rate, on the manufacturer's own spec page.

## How do you find the weak link in your own setup?

Work through the chain in the order that costs you the least.

> [!ACTION] Diagnose in this order
> 1. **Try DisplayPort if both ends have it.** On a PC this resolves the question in seconds and skips the HDMI problem entirely.
> 2. **Check the source device's stated bandwidth.** Look on the manufacturer's spec page for a Gbps figure or a maximum resolution and refresh rate. Not the version number.
> 3. **Check the display's input spec.** Many monitors and TVs have one high-bandwidth port and several slower ones. Ports are frequently not equal, and the fast one is rarely the one you plugged into.
> 4. **Check the cable's certification.** The official cable name must be printed on the jacket, and the packaging carries a QR-scannable certification label.
> 5. **Check DSC is enabled** in your GPU control panel, and that the monitor's HDMI input is set to its full-bandwidth mode. Many displays ship with that mode off by default under a name like "HDMI Enhanced" or "Input Signal Plus".

Step 3 catches more cases than any other. Step 5 catches most of the rest — a display whose enhanced-input toggle is off will negotiate a slow link no matter what hardware you attached.

And once you do get 144Hz negotiated, remember that refresh rate is only one of the three numbers that decide whether motion looks clean. The other two are frame rate and pixel response, which is a separate argument entirely — see [why a monitor advertised at 1ms rarely measures 1ms](/displays/monitor-response-time-1ms-explained/).

## Which cable should you actually buy?

If you need one, buy a certified **Ultra High Speed HDMI Cable** and stop there. It is certified to 48 Gbps, it covers everything a current consumer display can accept, it is backward compatible, and it is tested for low EMI so it does not interfere with nearby wireless gear.

What makes it trustworthy is the certification programme rather than the price. HDMI LA requires every cable model and every length to pass testing at an HDMI Forum Authorized Test Center. Certified cables must carry a label with a QR code you can scan to confirm that specific model passed. HDMI LA also runs a continuous audit: it buys cables from shops and marketplaces and re-tests them, and a model that fails cannot order more labels until the manufacturer fixes it.

> [!TIP] Scan the QR code on the packaging before you throw the box away. It returns the brand, model and length that were actually certified. An unscannable label, or no label at all, on a cable advertising 48 Gbps tells you what you need to know.

Ultra96 cables exist and carry 96 Gbps, but for 4K 144Hz they solve a problem you do not have. Buy one when you own hardware that needs it.

## The part nobody tells you

Here is the thing worth remembering after you have fixed this: the HDMI version number on a box is a marketing family, not a measurement. Neither is "Ultra96". Neither, for that matter, is the word "certified" on an unbranded listing with no scannable label.

The numbers that mean something are the ones with a testable definition behind them — 48 Gbps on a certified cable, a stated maximum refresh rate on a manufacturer spec page, a DSC ratio published by VESA. Everything else is a name.
